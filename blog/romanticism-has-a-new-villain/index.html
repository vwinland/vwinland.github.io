Romanticism Has a New Villain

A24 fans are cancelling their AAA24 memberships. The reason: a $75 million research partnership with Google DeepMind, aimed mostly at AI-assisted storyboarding. Scott Belsky, the A24 partner overseeing it, tried to get ahead of the backlash before it landed. The new tools, he told the Wall Street Journal, "won't look anything like the prompted generation type of AI that people feel uncomfortable with." It didn't matter. Reddit is in open mourning. "Membership cancelled" emails are being screenshotted like trophies.

This is not A24's first brush with this reaction, and it won't be the last anyone has. Martin Scorsese spent decades building his own storyboards by hand, then started using an AI tool called FLUX, from a startup called Black Forest Labs, to do it faster on his next film. The animator Sam Deats put the objection bluntly: storyboarding takes him seconds, so there's no reason to lean on a tool trained on other people's work. Guillermo del Toro went further months earlier, telling The Hollywood Reporter that the idea of generative AI touching his films would be "like spitting on God."

Strip away the specific technology and you've heard this argument before. You've heard it for two hundred years.

The reflex is older than the camera

Romanticism didn't appear out of nowhere. It rose as a direct response to the Industrial Revolution and the Enlightenment rationalism that preceded it — a defense of nature, emotion, and the individual against a world being reorganized around mechanization and measurable output. Wordsworth wrote odes to the healing power of nature as an escape from industrial pressure. Blake called the factories "dark satanic mills." The argument, even then, was never really about machines. It was about where the soul of a thing goes when a process replaces a person.

One thing the backlash often blurs together deserves its own line. The 2023 Hollywood writers' and actors' strikes were a real fight with real stakes — credit, residuals, and whether scripts and performances would be used to train the systems meant to replace the people who made them. That's a labor dispute with teeth. A director testing visualization tools during preproduction is a different question entirely, and treating both as the same fight is exactly the kind of collapse that turns a legitimate grievance into a moral panic that swallows everything adjacent to it.

Cucumber frames and Crystal Palaces

The clearest historical rerun is the Arts and Crafts movement. In 1851, the Great Exhibition opened in London's Crystal Palace, a vast iron-and-glass hall built to showcase the wonders of industrial manufacturing. It drew six million visitors. It also drew John Ruskin's contempt — he nicknamed the building the "cucumber frame," disgusted by what mass production had done to the quality and integrity of design. Ruskin's writing, especially The Stones of Venice, became the philosophical bedrock for William Morris and the Arts and Crafts movement that followed: a deliberate return to handmade goods, a rejection of the machine as something that hollowed out both the object and the worker who used to make it by hand.

The part that usually gets left out when this story is told as a clean morality tale: Ruskin's own position on machines wasn't consistent — he called machine production "altogether an evil" in some moods and hired machine-using manufacturers in others, as long as the quality held. Morris was similar. He didn't reject mechanization outright; he rejected what it had done to craftsmanship when nobody was paying attention to the difference. The founders of the movement most associated with anti-machine purism were, in practice, more interested in where judgment and care sat in the process than in the tool itself.

A generation later, the Bauhaus looked at the same tension and reached the opposite conclusion. Walter Gropius didn't see mass production as the enemy of design — he saw it as the only way design could matter to anyone outside a wealthy elite. The Bauhaus kept the Arts and Crafts attention to good design in daily life but dropped its romance with the individually handmade luxury object, betting that machine production, used well, could deliver good design at the scale a society actually needs.

Two movements, one generation apart, looking at the same machine and reaching opposite verdicts — not because the machine changed, but because the argument was never really about the machine.

Tools amplify intent. They don't replace it.

Scorsese still drew his own storyboards for seventy years before he touched an AI tool. What he wanted from it wasn't a replacement for his vision — it was a faster way to get that vision out of his head and in front of his production designer, art director, and cinematographer. That's the same problem every visualization tool in film history has tried to solve, from hand-drawn storyboards to animatics to digital previs. The tool changes. The job — communicate what you see before you spend the money to build it — doesn't.

None of this makes the discomfort irrational. The romantic reflex is protecting something real: the fear that judgment, taste, and irreproducibility — the things that make a film feel authored rather than assembled — quietly migrate out of human hands while everyone's looking at the efficiency gains. That's a legitimate thing to watch for. It's just not a reason to treat every tool as the same threat, or to mistake a faster sketchpad for a replacement of the sketcher.

The historical pattern doesn't say the fear was always wrong. It says the tool usually gets absorbed into the craft, not the other way around — and the fights that look like they're about machines are almost always about something else: who gets to claim authorship once a process gets faster, and whether the speed was ever really the point.

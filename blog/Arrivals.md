# Arrivals

I’m sitting outside a college town cafe and it is too hot to be doing this. The iced coffee gave up ten minutes ago. I stayed anyway, because something about this table keeps handing me things to watch. 

An hour ago I had lunch with my wife. She works down here. She stepped out of her building and into the middle of my Thursday and we walked. No agenda. Just two people using their feet at the same time in the same direction. It was not an event. That’s what I keep turning over. It was not an event, and it was the best part of my day. 

Then the woman with the meter.

She parked, got out, and fed the meter with the seriousness of someone paying admission. Then she sat on the bench and did nothing. In 2026 doing nothing on a bench is almost an act of protest. She wasn’t on her phone. She was pointed at the corner like it owed her something. 

It owed her a daughter. The daughter showed up a few minutes later and the bench changed shape. I couldn’t hear them. I didn’t need to. There's a specific animation people get when the person they were waiting for actually appears. It starts in the hands. The mother’s hands went up before the rest of her did. 

She paid a meter to wait. That’s the part I can’t put down. She could have circled the block. She could have idled somewhere with the AC running. Instead she paid the city for the privilege of sitting still until her daughter arrived. Quarters as devotion. There should be a word for that. 

Then the car at the curb. 

A woman pulled up and got out on the driver’s side first. She walked around, opened the back, and unfolded a walker on the sidewalk. She did it in one motion. No fumbling. She has done this a hundred times, maybe a thousand, and her body has memorized the sequence: walker first, then the passenger door. Her mother, I’m guessing, though I’ll never know. The passenger door opened only after the walker stood ready, locked, positioned at the exact angle a person would need it. 

Nobody performed anything this afternoon. Nobody knew they were being watched by a sweaty woman at a cafe table. That’s what made it unbearable in the good way. The meter, the bench, the walker. Care with the audience removed. Care as a series of small mechanical steps that only look like nothing. 

I keep thinking about the order of operations. You only learn that order by loving someone long enough to get it wrong once. 

And I keep thinking about my wife walking out of her building. How arrival is most of it, maybe. Not the grand gesture. The showing up, on time, at the corner, with the meter paid, with the walker ready. The unremarkable machinery of it.

I don’t know what to do with this feeling. It’s not sadness, though it sits close to it. It’s not gratitude exactly, though gratitude is in the room. It’s something like being caught off guard by how much tenderness is happening in public, constantly, unannounced, while the rest of us check our phones. 

The heat hasn’t let up. The bench is empty now. The car with the walker is long gone, I’m still here, holding a watered-down coffee, feeling whatever this is. 

I hope I remember the order of operations when it’s my turn.

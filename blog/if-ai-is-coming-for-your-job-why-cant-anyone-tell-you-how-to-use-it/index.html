If AI Is Coming for Your Job, Why Can’t Anyone Tell You How to Use It?

I was walking the track at my local gym while I listened to a recent episode of The New York Times’ The Daily called “What A.I. Is Actually Doing to the Economy.” And now here’s this essay.

Ask someone how they feel about AI and they might flinch. Mixed feelings. Anxious. Love-hate. Amazing and job-threatening in the same breath. Roughly seven in ten Americans think AI will shrink the job market overall, according to a March 2026 Quinnipiac University poll. Far fewer, about three in ten, say they’re personally worried about their own job. That gap is close to the essay in miniature: people are certain about the abstraction and unsure what it has to do with them specifically, which is exactly the space where bad advice and worse products get sold.

Part of the anxiety is not really about the technology. It’s about the contradiction sitting right in the middle of how AI gets talked about at work: companies want you to believe this thing is powerful enough to justify eliminating your role, and simultaneously, nobody can tell you what to actually do with it in your job. Those two claims don’t fit together. One of them is wrong, or at least overstated, and I don’t think it’s the one making everyone nervous.

The convenient scapegoat

Start with the layoffs. Amazon cut thousands of jobs and cited AI. Block’s CEO tied a near-halving of the workforce to AI tools changing what it means to run a company. Take those explanations at face value and the picture looks stark: the machine is already here, already replacing people, already proven.

The trend behind those headlines is real. AI has been the single most-cited reason employers give for job cuts every month since March 2026, according to outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas, up sharply from a year earlier when it barely registered as a factor.

But being the top cited reason isn’t the same as being the true one, and that’s exactly where economists start raising their hands. Daniel Zhao, chief economist at Glassdoor, has said outright that a company citing AI for layoffs doesn’t mean AI is actually why they’re happening. Fabian Stephany, who studies AI and work at the Oxford Internet Institute, has used the word “scapegoating” for the same pattern. A company that says “we’re making big AI gains,” gets rewarded by investors right now. A CEO who over-hired during the boom years has a much easier story to tell if the headline is “AI made us more efficient” than “I misjudged demand two years ago.” That doesn't mean AI is a fake factor. It means the citation and the cause aren’t guaranteed to be the same thing, and it’s always the citation a company has the strongest incentive to lead with. A layoff announcement is not a measurement of what AI did to the economy. It’s a measurement of what a company currently finds convenient to say about itself.

Nobody can tell you how to learn it

Somewhere in that episode, the host admitted something almost in passing: he’s still bad at using AI himself. He tries it for pulling up an old speech, building a contact list, gets frustrated partway through, and goes back to making phone calls. It wasn’t a gotcha moment, but an honest one in the conversation, and it stuck with me because it’s not an isolated case. He was describing his own job, on air, while reporting on whether the technology is about to upend everyone else’s.

Friends ask me, someone who works with these tools daily, how they should “learn to use AI.” I never have a clean answer, and the reason bothers me. When someone asked how to learn a spreadsheet program twenty years ago, or a coding language, or social media marketing, there was a real path: a course, a certification, a specific skill mapped to a specific task. “Learn AI” doesn’t have an equivalent path, because it isn’t really one skill. My honest first question back is always some version of, what are you actually trying to do this week? Because there’s no single tool, no fixed workflow, no on-ramp I can point to in the abstract. There’s only whatever task is sitting in front of you, and whether this thing happens to help with it.

The most common piece of concrete advice that circulates in offices right now is a manager telling someone to use AI to draft their emails. I would bet, if you put a suggestion box in front of actual employees and asked what they wanted help with, “write more emails, faster” would not be anywhere near the top of that pile. It’s advice invented by the people handing down the pressure to adopt, not advice requested by the people expected to adopt it, and that gap is close to the entire operational advice economy around a technology that, depending on which headline you catch that week, is either making everyone more productive or coming for entire departments.

Sit with that gap. The same institutions telling workers this technology is significant enough to justify layoffs can’t produce a coherent answer to “okay, so what do I actually do with it in my job.” If AI were as central to daily productivity as the layoff announcements imply, there would be an obvious first move to make, the way there was for spreadsheets or email itself. The vagueness isn’t a sign the technology is too advanced to explain simply. It’s a sign the story being told about it, that AI has already arrived as an unavoidable replacement for your specific job, is running ahead of what anyone can actually demonstrate you should do with it on Monday morning.

Even people who’ve already started using it aren’t sure what they're becoming. One person in that same episode joked that we’re all turning into the guy from Office Space who just claims to be “a people person,” except now the job is taking a business requirement and feeding it to the AI instead of doing the work yourself. It’s a funny line and also a real description of a role nobody applied for: not AI-skilled, not replaced, just a translator standing between a request and a machine, with no clearer sense of what mastery would even look like than anyone who hasn’t started.

Where you actually start

If you’re non-technical and the honest thought in your head is “I don’t want to be replaced, so I guess I should use this thing,” the uncomfortable thing is no one at your company is going to hand you real onboarding for it. Organizations want adoption numbers. They want to say employees are using AI. Almost none of them have built out what actually using it for a real task looks like, because that doesn’t compress into a training deck the way a new expense system does. So people teach themselves, badly, in the exact gap that the correspondent was describing about his own job.

Start with one task you already do repeatedly and don’t enjoy. Not a hypothetical. A real, recurring piece of your job: turning notes into a status update, summarizing a call, drafting a new document. Or it doesn't have to be work at all. Rehearsing a hard conversation before you have it, going back and forth on how to phrase something to a person, works the same way. So does handing it a few specific, competing options, three job offers, a few insurance plans, a couple apartments, with your actual details plugged in, and asking what the real tradeoffs are, instead of asking something generic.

The common thread across all of it: you’re handing it something that already exists, your notes, your options, your confusion, and asking it to reshape or clarify that, not asking it to be right about a fact it doesn’t have in front of it. That’s the category where it’s reliable. Open a general chat tool and try it on one of those, using your actual inputs, not a demo. Treat it like handing the task to a fast, literal new hire who has zero context about your job unless you give it some: say what the output should look like, show an example of good output if you have one, expect the first draft to need editing. The learning happens in the back-and-forth, watching what it gets wrong and adjusting what you tell it, not in a tutorial video.

That’s genuinely the whole method. Pick a real task. Try it on a real task. Adjust based on what comes back. Repeat with a second task once the first one feels natural. There’s no shortcut past that, because the tool doesn't have a fixed interface to master the way a spreadsheet does. The skill is closer to delegation than software literacy, and delegation gets learned by delegating, not by reading about it.

What actually works, modestly

Being honest about the other side of this, the same instinct that makes me distrust the layoff headlines should make me distrust flat pessimism just as much. People are getting real, small value out of this, and none of it requires believing anything dramatic.

The one I can actually vouch for, because it’s mine. As a writer, I sometimes have so many thoughts in my head at once that I need to just dump them all out, unsorted, before any of them turn into something. AI is genuinely good at taking that info dump and helping me organize it, dive into which threads are worth pulling on, and turn a mess into a draft. I’ve written more, and more consistently, since I started using it this way than at almost any other point.

I can also see that same mechanism, sorting a mental mess into something usable, applying to a hard email or similarly loaded piece of writing, or to something like running a dense insurance policy or lease through it and asking what it actually means in plain language before calling a lawyer. At work, there’s a more mundane version too: turning twenty minutes of messy meeting notes into an organized recap in ninety seconds, freeing that time for something that actually needed a person.

None of that is economy transforming. It’s a handful of ordinary tasks getting a little less tedious for a specific person on a specific day. That’s a much smaller claim than either “this changes everything” or “this is coming for your job,”and it’s one I can stand behind: one from watching it happen to my own work, the rest from reasoning honestly about where the same mechanism can apply.

The vagueness benefits someone

The gap between “AI is powerful enough to replace you” and “here’s how to actually use it” doesn’t go underutilized. It keeps the pressure to adopt in place without the cost of actually teaching anyone how. Pressure without a path produces compliance. Competence would take a training budget. Compliance doesn’t.

The real question isn’t “will AI take your job,” which nobody, including the people whose job it is to know, can answer with any precision right now. It’s why an industry confident enough to justify layoffs over this technology still can’t produce a straight answer to the much smaller question of how a person gets good at using it.

It’s not just current workers absorbing the gap, either. One listener in the episode’s closing montage said her grandsons are heading into fifth and seventh grade, and she has no idea what they’ll be choosing among, or what will already be gone, by the time they’re old enough to pick a path. The vagueness reaches a generation that hasn’t even started yet.
